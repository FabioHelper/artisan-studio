## 1. USDA FoodData Central (Foundation, SR Legacy, FNDDS, Branded): coverage, license, API limits, bulk downloads, portion weights

### Takeaway
FoodData Central (FDC) is the strongest free base for mass-to-macro conversion. It is public domain (CC0 1.0), so commercial use needs no attribution or share-alike. The API is free with a data.gov key and allows 1,000 requests/hour per IP, and every dataset can be bulk-downloaded as CSV/JSON. FNDDS 2021-2023 adds about 22,000 portion weights (cup, tbsp, "1 medium", and so on) that turn household measures into grams. That makes it the most useful free volume-to-mass resource for prepared or mixed dishes.

### Cited Findings
- "USDA FoodData Central data are in the public domain and they are not copyrighted. They are published under CC0 1.0 Universal (CC0 1.0)." — [FDC API Guide](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/api-guide) (via search summary)
- API rate limit: default 1,000 requests per hour per IP address. Going over it blocks the API key for 1 hour. Every request needs a data.gov API key, and anyone may get one. — [FDC API Guide](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/api-guide); OpenAPI spec at [fdc_api.html](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/api-spec/fdc_api.html)
- Bulk downloads for all data types (Foundation, SR Legacy, FNDDS/Survey, Branded, Experimental) are listed at — [FDC Download Datasets](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/download-datasets). I could not fetch this page, so current file sizes and release dates are unverified.
- SR Legacy came out in April 2018 as the last release of that data type and will not be updated. — [FDC Foundation Foods Documentation](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/Foundation_Foods_Documentation)
- Foundation Foods covers basic, unprocessed or lightly processed foods and includes extensive metadata: "the number of samples, sampling location, date of collection, and analytical approaches used". — [FDC Foundation Foods Documentation](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/Foundation_Foods_Documentation)
- The April 2026 FDC release was planned to add Foundation Foods for highly consumed basic foods and ingredients: mayonnaise, salad dressings, peanut butter, condensed milk, edamame, canned beans, cranberries, grapefruit, prunes and raisins. — [FDC Future Updates](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/future-updates). This is the "planned" wording. I could not confirm that everything shipped.
- FNDDS 2021-2023 is "an application database designed to convert food and beverage portions reported in What We Eat in America, NHANES into gram amounts and to determine their nutrient values". It "contains approximately 22,000 weights for portions of foods" and converts household measures ("a medium apple, 2 slices of bread, and a can of soda") into grams. — [FNDDS 2021-2023 At a Glance (USDA ARS)](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400530/pdf/fndds/2021_2023_At_A_Glance.pdf); [FNDDS 2021-2023 Documentation](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400530/pdf/fndds/2021_2023_FNDDS_Doc.pdf); [FNDDS download page](https://www.ars.usda.gov/northeast-area/beltsville-md-bhnrc/beltsville-human-nutrition-research-center/food-surveys-research-group/docs/fndds-download-databases/)
- The FNDDS 2021-2023 enhancements are described in a peer-reviewed paper (Morton et al., J. Food Composition and Analysis, 2025). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157525009822)
- SR Legacy household-measure example that shows how much density varies among visually similar foods: 1 cup = 47 g romaine, 36 g green leaf, 28 g red leaf, 55 g butterhead, 72 g iceberg lettuce (UF/IFAS citing USDA SR Legacy). — [UF/IFAS HS1416](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/HS1416)
- Branded Foods scale: one aggregator describes FDC as "Nutrient Data for 300K+ Foods". This is a secondary source; take the exact Branded count from the FDC download page. — [Better Analyst (aggregator)](https://betteranalyst.com/datasets/usda-food-nutrition-data)
- DietAI24 grounded multimodal-LLM food recognition in FNDDS through retrieval-augmented generation instead of the model's internal knowledge. It reported a 63% reduction in mean absolute error for food weight and four key nutrients on real-world mixed dishes, which shows FNDDS's portion and recipe structure is useful as a lookup backbone. — [DietAI24, Communications Medicine (Nature) 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-025-01159-0)

### Inferences
- Recommended FDC stack: Foundation Foods first for raw or single-ingredient foods (analytical data with sample metadata), then SR Legacy for breadth (frozen since 2018 but around 7.8k foods), then FNDDS for "as eaten" and mixed dishes plus portion weights, then Branded only for barcode matches. CC0 means all of it can be bundled into an app offline without attribution, though attribution is still good practice.
- FNDDS portion weights (grams per cup, tbsp, piece) are empirical "grams per household volume" values, so they act as bulk densities for amorphous foods (rice, mashed potatoes, stews, salads). For a camera-volume pipeline, dividing FNDDS "1 cup" grams by 236.6 mL gives an as-served bulk density for about the same foods the nutrient lookup already uses. That keeps density and composition entries consistent, which is an advantage over mixing FAO density entries with separate nutrient entries.
- 1,000 requests/hour per IP is too low to serve a consumer app live. Ship a local copy built from the bulk CSV/JSON downloads instead of calling the API at runtime.

### Gaps
- Exact current counts (Foundation Foods items, FNDDS foods and nutrients, Branded items) and whether a late-2026 release happened could not be verified because fdc.nal.usda.gov was unreachable. From memory (unverified): FNDDS 2021-2023 has about 5,400 foods and about 65 nutrients/components, and SR Legacy has about 7,793 foods.
- Whether the FDC "Terms of Service" add conditions for Branded Foods (the data comes from industry and GS1/label data) beyond the general CC0 statement was not verified.

## 2. Brazilian tables: TACO (NEPA/UNICAMP), TBCA (USP/FoRC), IBGE POF

### Takeaway
TACO (4th ed., 2011, 597 foods) is free and may be reproduced "in whole or in part" with the source cited, so it is the safest Brazilian table for commercial bundling. It is small and dated, though, and its "cozido" entries appear to be cooked without the oil Brazilians normally use. TBCA (v7.2, over 5,700 foods including over 4,000 preparations) fits Brazilian home cooking much better, but its terms forbid commercialization and alteration and require contacting the coordinators for commercial use. Using TBCA in a paid app therefore needs a license agreement. IBGE POF provides composition tables and household-measure tables built for Brazilian dietary recalls.

### Cited Findings
- TACO (Tabela Brasileira de Composição de Alimentos), NEPA/UNICAMP, 4th revised and expanded edition, Campinas-SP, 2011. It covers about 597 foods: raw ("in natura"), processed and typical preparations, with macros, fiber and some micronutrients. — [NEPA/UNICAMP TACO PDF page](https://nepa.unicamp.br/publicacoes/tabela-taco-pdf/); [TACO 4th ed. PDF (CFN mirror)](https://cfn.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/taco_4_edicao_ampliada_e_revisada.pdf)
- TACO license: "É permitida a reprodução total ou parcial do material, desde que seja citada a fonte" (full or partial reproduction allowed as long as the source is cited). It is free in PDF and Excel. — [NEPA/UNICAMP TACO](https://nepa.unicamp.br/publicacoes/tabela-taco-pdf/); [TACO Excel](https://nepa.unicamp.br/publicacoes/tabela-taco-excel/)
- Example TACO values per 100 g: arroz tipo 1 cozido 128 kcal, 2.5 g protein, 28.1 g carbohydrate, 0.2 g lipids. Feijão carioca cozido 76 kcal, 4.8 g protein, 13.6 g carbohydrate, 0.5 g lipids. — [calculadoradecalorias.com.br (TACO mirror) rice](https://calculadoradecalorias.com.br/tabela-nutricional/taco/arroz-tipo-1-cozido); [beans](https://calculadoradecalorias.com.br/tabela-nutricional/taco/feijao-carioca-cozido) (third-party mirrors of TACO; check against the NEPA Excel)
- TBCA v7.2: "dados de mais de 5700 alimentos, sendo mais de 4000 preparações" (data on more than 5,700 foods, of which more than 4,000 are preparations), including texture-modified, gluten-free, lactose-free and vegan/vegetarian preparations. — [TBCA](https://www.tbca.net.br/) (via search summary); news of the new version at [CFN](https://cfn.org.br/ja-esta-no-ar-a-nova-versao-da-tabela-brasileira-de-composicao-de-alimentos-tbca/)
- TBCA terms of use, as summarized in search results: commercial purposes require contacting the coordinators; citing the source is mandatory; "Não é permitida a comercialização" (commercialization not allowed); total or partial alteration of the content is not allowed. — [TBCA](https://www.tbca.net.br/); also [FoRC TBCA page](https://forc.net.br/tabela-brasileira-de-composicao-de-alimentos-tbca/)
- TBCA published a 2025 data-aggregation manual ("manual para agregação de dados"), so the table is still actively maintained. — [TBCA manual_agregacao_2025.pdf](https://www.tbca.net.br/arquivosestaticos/manual_agregacao_2025.pdf)
- IBGE POF 2008-2009 published "Tabelas de composição nutricional dos alimentos consumidos no Brasil". — [IBGE POF 2008-2009 composition tables](https://censoagro2017.ibge.gov.br/1992-novo-portal/edicao/9063-2008-2009-tabelas-de-composicao-nutricional-dos-alimentos-consumidos-no-brasil-pof2009.html)
- IBGE POF 2017-2018 "Análise do Consumo Alimentar Pessoal" used two non-consecutive days of food records for residents aged 10 and over. It recorded food name, type of preparation, measure used and quantity consumed, so it relies on a household-measure-to-gram and preparation-to-nutrient mapping for Brazilian foods. — [IBGE POF 2017-2018 consumption analysis](https://censoagro2017.ibge.gov.br/1992-novo-portal/edicao/28523-analise-do-consumo-alimentar-pessoal-no-brasil-pof2017-2018.html); [IBGE POF 2017-2018 portal](https://www.ibge.gov.br/estatisticas/sociais/saude/24786-pof-2017-2018.html)

### Inferences
- Licensing for a commercial Brazilian app: TACO can be bundled with attribution. TBCA cannot be bundled or redistributed commercially without explicit permission from the USP/FoRC coordinators. The "no alteration" clause probably also bars deriving new values from it, such as re-computing preparations. IBGE publications are government statistics that are usually freely reusable with attribution, but I did not verify their license.
- TACO's cooked rice has 0.2 g fat/100 g, which suggests it was cooked without oil. Typical Brazilian home rice is sautéed in oil with garlic and onion first, and TBCA's large preparations set exists to capture that. Using TACO "cozido" for home arroz/feijão will probably under-count fat and kcal. This is the "hidden fat" problem (Section 5), and it matters most for Brazilian staples.
- Practical approach: map Brazilian labels to TACO for raw and simple items (commercially safe), and pursue a TBCA license for preparations. Where no license is obtained, recipe-calculate preparations from TACO/FDC ingredients plus a Brazilian default oil or salt amount.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full TBCA terms page, so it is unclear whether an API or bulk export exists (the public site appears to be a web lookup) and what TBCA's commercial licensing costs or conditions are.
- I did not confirm the IBGE "Tabela de Medidas Referidas para os Alimentos Consumidos no Brasil" (POF 2008-2009 household-measure-to-gram table), its license or format. It probably exists and would be the Brazilian equivalent of FNDDS portion weights. It is worth checking directly on ibge.gov.br.
- I found no authoritative statement on whether TACO will get a 5th edition.

## 3. Open Food Facts: license and usefulness as a packaged-food / barcode fallback

### Takeaway
Open Food Facts (OFF) allows commercial use under the ODbL. Any public use requires attribution, and any adapted or derived database that is used publicly must be released under the ODbL. Images are CC BY-SA. With over 3 million products it is the natural barcode fallback for packaged foods. Its values are crowd-sourced copies of labels, and labels themselves may legally deviate by 20% (FDA and ANVISA).

### Cited Findings
- OFF data is licensed under the Open Database License (ODbL), and artwork/images under Creative Commons Attribution–Share Alike. — [Wikipedia: Open Food Facts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Food_Facts); [OFF Terms of use](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/terms-of-use)
- ODbL permits any purpose, including commercial, with attribution (e.g., "Contains data from Open Food Facts, available under the Open Database License"). If you publicly use an adapted version of the database, or works produced from an adapted database, you must offer that adapted database under the ODbL. — [OFF Terms of use](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/terms-of-use); discussion in [DEV Community: What ODbL Means for Commercial Nutrition Apps](https://dev.to/dietly/what-odbl-means-for-commercial-nutrition-apps-4hl7); API conditions at [OFF Forum](https://forum.openfoodfacts.org/t/conditions-to-use-the-open-food-facts-api/443)
- As of 2026, OFF has over 3 million product entries from more than 200 countries. — [Wikipedia: Open Food Facts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Food_Facts)
- Data dumps, API and SDKs are listed at — [OFF Data](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/data)
- US FDA label tolerance (21 CFR 101.9(g)): for naturally occurring (Class II) nutrients the measured value must be at least 80% of the label value. For calories, total fat, saturated fat, sodium, total carbohydrate and sugars, the actual value must not exceed the declared value by more than 20%. — [FDA Guide for Developing and Using Data Bases for Nutrition Labeling](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/guidance-industry-guide-developing-and-using-data-bases-nutrition-labeling)
- Brazil, ANVISA RDC 429/2020 (art. 33): energy, carbohydrates, total/added sugars, total/saturated/trans fats, sodium and cholesterol may not exceed the declared value by more than 20%. Protein, fiber, mono/polyunsaturated fats, vitamins and minerals may not be more than 20% below the declared value. — [RDC 429/2020 (BVS/MS)](https://bvsms.saude.gov.br/bvs/saudelegis/anvisa/2020/RDC_429_2020_.pdf); [Conass summary](https://www.conass.org.br/conass-informa-n-392-2020-publicada-a-rdc-anvisa-n-429-que-dispoe-sobre-a-rotulagem-nutricional-dos-alimentos-embalados/)

### Inferences
- If OFF records are used only as a runtime lookup (per-barcode, not merged into your own database), the obligation is attribution. If you merge, clean and redistribute OFF data, or publicly use a database derived from it, share-alike applies. Keep OFF data in a separate store from proprietary data to avoid "adapted database" questions (get a legal opinion).
- A camera app cannot read a barcode from a plated meal, so OFF helps only with packaged items photographed in their packaging, or as a manual "scan the package" add-on. For packaged items, the ±20% label tolerance sets a floor on how accurate the kcal figure can be.

### Gaps
- Count of Brazil-specific products in OFF, and completeness of their nutrition fields, was not found.
- OFF API rate limits (current numbers) were not verified because the page was blocked.

## 4. Density (volume → mass): FAO/INFOODS Density Database v2.0 and density variance for common foods

### Takeaway
The FAO/INFOODS Density Database v2.0 (2012) is the standard free density reference: 638 entries from 11 sources, in g/mL, with density, bulk density and specific gravity defined separately. It is an Excel file. Practical bulk density for amorphous plated foods varies a lot with packing and preparation. Cooked rice ranges about 130-170 g per cup (±13%), and leafy greens range 28-72 g per cup depending on type (2.6×). So even a perfect volume estimate can carry roughly 10-25% mass error for such foods unless the food subtype is identified.

### Cited Findings
- The FAO/INFOODS Density Database is "a tool for converting volume to weight and vice versa" and contains density data for 638 food entries from 11 sources. Version 2 adds specific gravity data and defines terms (density, bulk density, specific gravity). Values are given to two decimals, and literature values to three. — [FAO/INFOODS Density Database v2.0 PDF](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/density_DB_v2_0_01.pdf); [FAO catalogue entry](https://www.fao.org/food-composition/tables-and-databases/detail/(global--2012)-fao-infoods-density-database---version-2/en); [FAO Open Knowledge repository](https://openknowledge.fao.org/items/516facd8-e460-4869-b0d4-771295d1ff38); [USDA ARS record](https://www.ars.usda.gov/research/publications/publication/?seqNo115=286890)
- Excel download: [density_DB_v2_0_final-1__1_.xlsx](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/density_DB_v2_0_final-1__1_.xlsx). It covers beverages, dairy, oils and fats, cereals, fruits, vegetables and other groups. — (via search summary of the FAO page)
- FAO also publishes the "INFOODS Guidelines for Food Matching v1.2", which covers matching foods across databases. — [FAO INFOODS Food Matching Guidelines](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/upload/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Leafy greens, 1 cup (USDA SR Legacy): romaine 47 g, green leaf 36 g, red leaf 28 g, butterhead 55 g, iceberg 72 g. — [UF/IFAS HS1416](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/HS1416)
- Cooked rice: "a 'cup' of cooked rice can fluctuate between 130g and 170g depending on how fluffy or packed it is". Another source gives about 155-165 g per cup (about 0.65-0.70 g/mL). Short-grain packs tighter than long-grain basmati or jasmine. Measuring cups "can vary by 10-20% depending on how you pack or level the ingredient". — [Chef's Resource](https://www.chefsresource.com/how-many-grams-in-1-cup-cooked-rice/); [CookConvert](https://cookconvertapp.com/cups-to-grams/rice/) (non-authoritative cooking sites, used for scale only; USDA SR Legacy/FNDDS cup weights are the authoritative values)
- A systematic review of AI image-based dietary assessment against ground truth found relative errors of 0.09-33% for volume and 0.10-38.3% for calories. — [AI-based digital image dietary assessment vs humans and ground truth: systematic review (PMC10836267)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10836267/)

### Inferences
- A density lookup needs to distinguish (a) true density of solid pieces (meat, potato: around 1.0-1.1 g/mL, close to water) from (b) as-served bulk density of piled or particulate foods (rice, salad, fries, farofa), which depends on void fraction. Camera volume (a convex hull or height map) measures the bulk envelope, so bulk density is the right quantity for piled foods. FNDDS/SR "1 cup" weights are better bulk-density proxies than the FAO true-density values for that case.
- Rough error from density alone, even with perfect volume: about ±5% for dense solid items (meat, bread, whole fruit), about ±10-15% for cooked grains (packing), and up to 2× for leafy salads if the lettuce type is misidentified. For Brazilian plates, farofa (very low bulk density) and feijão (liquid-rich, near 1 g/mL) are extremes worth special-casing.
- Raw vs cooked rice: grains absorb water, so cooked rice mass is roughly 2.5-3× raw dry mass (typical cooking ratio, not sourced here). Confusing a raw-rice density or nutrient entry with a cooked one gives a roughly 3× kcal-per-gram error. The label must always carry the cooked state.

### Gaps
- I could not open the FAO spreadsheet, so specific FAO density values for cooked rice, beans, chicken breast and salads are not quoted. Pull them from the XLSX before use.
- The license terms of the FAO Density Database are not stated in the search results. FAO content is usually under CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO, which would restrict commercial use, but this was not verified for this dataset and is an important check. Density values themselves are facts, and deriving your own values from FNDDS (CC0) avoids the question.
- I found no peer-reviewed study that quantifies bulk-density variance per food across home-prepared servings. The rice figures above come from cooking sites.

## 5. Cooking: yield/retention factors, cooked vs raw, oil absorption, hidden fats and sauces

### Takeaway
USDA publishes free cooking-yield tables for meat and poultry (Release 2, 2014, CSV) and nutrient-retention factors (Release 6, 2007). Retention factors cover vitamins and minerals, not macros. Macro changes on cooking are mostly mass changes: water loss concentrates nutrients per gram, while fat drips or is absorbed. The largest macro errors at this stage come from fat that cannot be seen. Deep-fried foods can take up oil to 14% (fries) and up to 40% (chips) of their weight, and added cooking oil and dressings are the most consistently under-reported energy source.

### Cited Findings
- USDA Table of Cooking Yields for Meat and Poultry, Release 2: percentages for cooking yield, moisture change and fat change for specific cuts prepared under USDA protocols. Yields describe "changes in food weight due to moisture loss (e.g., evaporation or moisture drip), water absorption (e.g., boiling) or fat gains/losses". — [USDA Cooking Yields Release 2 PDF](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400535/Data/retn/USDA_CookingYields_MeatPoultry02.pdf); CSV and data dictionary at [Ag Data Commons](https://agdatacommons.nal.usda.gov/articles/dataset/USDA_Table_of_Cooking_Yields_for_Meat_and_Poultry/24660864) and [data.gov catalog](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/usda-table-of-cooking-yields-for-meat-and-poultry-b07c4)
- USDA's Nutrient Data Laboratory applies cooking yields and/or retention factors to recipes to turn raw-ingredient values into cooked-food values in SR. — [USDA Cooking Yields Release 2](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400535/Data/retn/USDA_CookingYields_MeatPoultry02.pdf)
- USDA Table of Nutrient Retention Factors, Release 6 (2007): retention factors for 16 vitamins and 8 minerals (plus alcohol) for about 290 foods under specified preparation methods. It replaced Release 5 (2003). — [Retention Factors Release 6 PDF](https://www.ars.usda.gov/arsuserfiles/80400530/pdf/retn06.pdf); [data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/usda-table-of-nutrient-retention-factors-release-6-2007); [Ag Data Commons](https://agdatacommons.nal.usda.gov/articles/dataset/USDA_Table_of_Nutrient_Retention_Factors_Release_6_2007_/24660888)
- EuroFIR/Bell et al. report on nutrient loss and gain factors used in European food-composition databases for recipe calculation. — [Bell et al. (LanguaL site)](http://www.langual.org/Download/RecipeCalculation/Bell%20et%20al%20-%20Report%20on%20Nutrient%20Losses%20and%20Gains%20Factors%20used%20in%20European%20Food%20Composition%20Databases.pdf); [EuroFIR Compilers' Toolbox – Recipe Calculation](http://toolbox.foodcomp.info/ToolBox_RecipeCalculation.asp)
- Oil uptake in deep-frying: french-fry fat content can go from 0.2% to 14%, potato chips can reach 40% fat, and "oil can constitute over one-third of the total weight of fried foods". Thinner products with more surface absorb more oil (fries absorb less than chips). For battered foods, batter formulation and frying time drive oil distribution. — [Review of frying oils and oleogels for fat-uptake reduction (Heliyon/ScienceDirect, 2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S240584402308708X); [Review of oil-uptake mechanisms (IJFST)](https://academic.oup.com/ijfst/article/43/8/1410/7864485)
- Hidden fats: "the foods most commonly under-reported or omitted from food diaries are condiments, cooking fats, and beverages". A commercial blog claims trackers under-estimate by 400-600 kcal/day with roughly half from oils and dressings. That claim is not from a peer-reviewed source and should not be quoted as fact. — [Nutrola blog (commercial, low reliability)](https://nutrola.app/en/blog/the-hidden-calories-you-dont-know-youre-eating); [CalEye blog (commercial)](https://caleye.fit/blog/hidden-calories-dressings-sauces-oils/)

### Inferences
- Cooked vs raw (meat): if the vision model sees cooked chicken but the lookup uses a raw entry, protein and kcal per gram are under-estimated by roughly the inverse of the cooking yield. Grilled chicken breast typically loses about 25-30% of its mass as water (typical yield about 70-75%, to be confirmed in the USDA yields CSV), so the error is about 30-40%. Always map to "cooked, [method]" entries, which FDC SR/FNDDS and TACO/TBCA provide for common items.
- Oil arithmetic: fat is about 9 kcal/g (Atwater). One tablespoon of oil is about 13-14 g, so about 120 kcal. On a 600 kcal Brazilian lunch plate, 1 tbsp of unseen oil in the rice and beans refogado is roughly a 20% energy error, almost all of it fat. A plate photo cannot see it, so the only practical mitigation is priors: default "as typically prepared" entries such as FNDDS or TBCA preparations, plus a user toggle for "fried / with oil / dressing".
- Deep-fried items: oil content depends on thickness and surface area, so the same visual volume of thin chips vs thick fries can differ 3× in fat per gram (14% vs 40% fat). Fried items should map to specific "fried" database entries, not to raw ingredients plus a generic oil adder.
- Sauces and mixed dishes (feijoada, strogonoff, lasagna): composition can't be seen from the top surface. The best available approach is a single "as eaten" recipe entry (FNDDS, TBCA preparations) rather than decomposing into visible ingredients.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed number for the share of energy from under-reported added fats in camera-based logging specifically. The popular "400-600 kcal/day" number is unsourced.
- I could not retrieve specific USDA yield values (e.g., chicken breast grilled) because the ars.usda.gov PDF was blocked. The yield ranges above are my estimates to be checked against the CSV.
- No Brazilian-specific data found on typical oil amounts in home arroz/feijão preparations. TBCA preparation recipes would contain this but are not openly licensed.

## 6. Intrinsic variance: how much nutrient values vary within the same named food (error floor)

### Takeaway
Even with perfect weight and correct identification, a single table value can't predict one specific portion exactly. Protein is relatively stable, but fat is the most variable macro (CVs above 20% in meat-type materials versus about 10% for protein). Calculated diets differ systematically from chemically analysed duplicates: calculated carbohydrate runs high, and the correlation for energy was only 0.57 in one controlled-trial study. Packaged-food labels may legally be off by 20%. A realistic floor is about ±5-10% kcal for staple carbohydrate foods and about ±15-30% for fat-variable foods such as meats and fried items.

### Cited Findings
- "Due to the natural variability of foodstuffs, food composition data may not predict the composition of a single portion of any particular food with accuracy." True variability comes from soil, fertilizer, weather, pest control, genetics, post-harvest physiology, storage and processing. — [Nutritional Assessment, Ch. 4 Food Composition (Gibson)](https://nutritionalassessment.org/chap04/); [Greenfield & Southgate, Food Composition Data (FAO, 2003)](https://www.fao.org/uploads/media/Greenfield_and_shouthgate_2003_English_02.pdf)
- Apples harvested at the same time from the same tree can differ more than two-fold in many micronutrients. For meat, "nutrient composition ... can vary greatly depending on the proportion of lean to fat tissue". Compositional variability "impedes the accurate assessment of intake", and results of many nutrition studies based on food composition data are "potentially unreliable". — [Ottaviani/Kuhnle et al., eLife 2024 "Reliance on self-reports and estimated food composition data ... introduces significant bias"](https://elifesciences.org/articles/92941); [PubMed 38896457](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38896457)
- Meat and bone meal (an animal-feed analog, not human food): protein CV 9.8%, while fat and ash CV exceeded 22%. — [Animal Bioscience](https://www.animbiosci.org/upload/pdf/15_239.pdf)
- Commercial meat products show year-round variability in proximate composition, and the variability was greater in lean-only cut products than in ground meats. — [Production variations of nutritional composition of commercial meat products (Food Research International)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0963996910003170)
- Plant-based meat analogues: energy and nutrient CV within the same category "in several cases approached or exceeded 100%". This is across products, not within one product. — [Comparative assessment of meat products and plant-based analogues (PMC10305646)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10305646/)
- Controlled feeding trials, 25 duplicate diets: calculated energy and macros (especially carbohydrate) were higher than chemically analysed values. Correlations ranged from 0.57 for energy to 1.00 for PUFA. Differences came from batch-to-batch ingredient variation and analytical uncertainty. — [Evaluation of calculated energy and macronutrient contents of diets by chemical analysis of duplicate portions (JFCA)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157515001271)
- Two South African food-composition databases compared against chemical analysis for controlled-feeding diets. — [PubMed 18088565](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18088565/)
- Label tolerance ±20% (FDA 21 CFR 101.9(g); ANVISA RDC 429/2020 art. 33). See Section 3. — [FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/guidance-industry-guide-developing-and-using-data-bases-nutrition-labeling); [RDC 429/2020](https://bvsms.saude.gov.br/bvs/saudelegis/anvisa/2020/RDC_429_2020_.pdf)
- Different food-composition tables give divergent intakes for the same diet. — [Evaluation of the food composition tables: beyond the divergence of intakes (PMC8189211)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8189211/); [Exploring two food composition databases to estimate nutritional components of whole meals (JFCA 2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157521002702)

### Inferences
- Error floor by macro: protein about ±10% (CV around 10% in the meat analog). Fat about ±20-30% or more (CV above 22%, and it dominates in meats and fried foods). Carbohydrate in starchy staples is probably tighter because it is set largely by water content, but cooking water varies and table values can run high. Energy follows fat in fatty foods and water content in starchy foods.
- Foundation Foods publishes the number of samples and analytical metadata. Where it gives min/max or SD, the app could propagate a per-food uncertainty instead of a fixed floor (Foundation Foods metadata as described in Section 1).
- Because fat is both the most variable nutrient and the most invisible (Section 5), confidence intervals should be widest on fat and kcal. That argues for showing ranges rather than point values in the UI.

### Gaps
- No single authoritative, citable table of CVs for protein, fat, carbohydrate and kcal within the same named foods (e.g., chicken breast, cooked rice) was found through search snippets. The Greenfield & Southgate FAO book and USDA Foundation Foods sample-level data are the places to compute these directly.
- The eLife paper's exact quantitative results (it focuses on bioactives like flavanols, nitrate and epicatechin) could not be extracted because the page was blocked.

## 7. Mapping a vision label ("grilled chicken breast", "arroz branco") to a nutrient entry without LLMs

### Takeaway
The main open food-description systems are FoodOn (CC BY 4.0, over 9,600 generic food categories, OBO Foundry), LanguaL (a free 14-facet thesaurus maintained by Danish Food Informatics) and EFSA FoodEx2 (a hierarchical classification with facets). FoodOn already has SSSOM mappings to FoodEx2, built by string matching of labels and synonyms plus shared LanguaL cross-references. That is the recommended non-LLM method. For a camera app, the most reliable approach is a hand-curated table from the vision model's closed label set to specific FDC/TACO IDs, with cooking method and state as explicit facets, in line with the INFOODS Food Matching Guidelines.

### Cited Findings
- FoodOn is licensed CC BY 4.0, repository [github.com/FoodOntology/foodon](https://github.com/FoodOntology/foodon), and is part of the OBO Foundry. — [OBO Foundry: FoodOn](https://obofoundry.org/ontology/foodon)
- FoodOn offers "a set of over 9,600 generic food product categories" and is largely based on LanguaL. — [FoodOn](https://foodon.org/); [FoodOn and LanguaL](https://foodon.org/design/foodon-and-langual/)
- LanguaL has 14 facets describing food source (plant/animal organism), preservation, cooking method, packaging, consumer group, labelling and more. It is "provided online as a free resource by Danish Food Informatics" and has been used to index EU and US agency databases. — [LanguaL](https://www.langual.org/); [Agrisemantics map](https://vest.agrisemantics.org/domains/food-and-human-nutrition)
- FoodEx2 is EFSA's food classification and description system, hierarchical and built for exposure assessment. — [FoodEx2 on AgroPortal](https://agroportal.eu/ontologies/FOODEX2); [FoodEx2 harmonization example (PMC11013267)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11013267/)
- FoodOn to FoodEx2 SSSOM mappings were "automatically discovered based on string matches in labels and synonyms, or based on shared cross-references to LanguaL". — [Zenodo: FoodOn to FoodEx2 maintenance 2021 mappings](https://zenodo.org/records/10716098)
- FoodOn has been reused to build a knowledge base of food-composition data. — [Griffiths et al., Semantic Web journal 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.3233/SW-233207)
- Database of LanguaL and FoodEx2 codes for 50 ready-to-eat products, a worked example of dual coding. — [PMC11054341](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11054341/)
- INFOODS Guidelines for Food Matching v1.2 give FAO's procedure for matching consumed foods to composition entries. — [FAO INFOODS](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/upload/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- A hierarchy-aware food classification approach for dietary assessment, useful for backing off to a parent category when a fine label is uncertain. — [Improving Dietary Assessment via Integrated Hierarchy Food Classification (arXiv 2109.02736)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.02736)
- LLM-based RAG over FNDDS (DietAI24) cut weight and nutrient MAE by 63% on mixed dishes, which shows that grounding in a database beats free-form nutrient guessing. It is included here as the comparison point, since it does use an LLM. — [DietAI24](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-025-01159-0)

### Inferences
- Non-LLM mapping recipe: (1) keep a closed label vocabulary in the vision model (e.g., about 200-500 classes weighted to Brazilian dishes). (2) Pre-map each class offline, once and by a human, to a primary nutrient ID (FDC fdcId or TACO code) with explicit facets for state (raw/cooked), method (grilled/fried/boiled) and "with oil" defaults. LanguaL facet names give a ready-made controlled vocabulary for these. (3) Use FoodOn IDs as a language-neutral pivot so "arroz branco" and "white rice, cooked" resolve to the same concept. (4) For open-vocabulary fallback, use string or synonym matching (FoodOn synonyms, Portuguese synonyms from TACO/TBCA names) plus hierarchy back-off to a parent category.
- Most mapping error comes from missing facets (cooked vs raw, fried vs grilled, with vs without skin or oil), not from synonyms. These facets change kcal/g by 30% to 3× (Sections 4-5), so the curated table should force a facet choice rather than a bare name.
- CC BY 4.0 (FoodOn) permits commercial use with attribution. I did not verify the LanguaL and FoodEx2 license terms.

### Gaps
- Explicit license terms for LanguaL and FoodEx2 were not found in search results.
- I found no published benchmark of non-LLM label-to-FCDB mapping accuracy (e.g., % top-1 correct match) for vision labels.
- No existing open mapping of TACO or TBCA codes to FoodOn or LanguaL was found.

## 8. Overall: how much error does the data/lookup stage add (volume → mass → macros)?

### Takeaway
With perfect volume and correct identification, the data stage alone plausibly adds about ±10-20% kcal error for simple staples (rice, beans, plain grilled meat) and ±25-50% or more for fried, sauced or oil-prepared and mixed dishes. The main contributors are bulk-density variance, fat variability and invisible added fat. End-to-end AI systems report calorie errors from about 0.1% up to 38%, and this stage is a meaningful part of that.

### Cited Findings
- AI image-based dietary assessment vs ground truth: calorie relative errors 0.10%-38.3%, and volume 0.09%-33%. — [Systematic review (PMC10836267)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10836267/)
- An energy-density-map approach reported 13.57% average energy error on Nutrition5k. Another system averaged 209 kcal error per eating occasion. — (search summary of) [End-to-end image-based food energy estimation protocol (PMC6521161)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6521161/) and [Image Based Food Energy Estimation With Depth Domain Adaptation (arXiv 2208.12153)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.12153). The attribution of each number to each paper was not verified.
- Mixed dishes, error accumulation across recognition and volume stages, and cuisine domain shift remain open problems. — [Comprehensive review of AI for food recognition and nutrient estimation (Discover AI, 2026)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44163-026-01526-3)
- Component ranges supporting the budget: bulk density of leafy greens 28-72 g/cup ([UF/IFAS](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/HS1416)); cooked rice about 130-170 g/cup ([Chef's Resource](https://www.chefsresource.com/how-many-grams-in-1-cup-cooked-rice/), non-authoritative); fat CV above 22% vs protein 9.8% ([Animal Bioscience](https://www.animbiosci.org/upload/pdf/15_239.pdf)); fried-food fat 14-40% of weight ([Heliyon review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S240584402308708X)); label tolerance ±20% ([RDC 429/2020](https://bvsms.saude.gov.br/bvs/saudelegis/anvisa/2020/RDC_429_2020_.pdf)).

### Inferences
- An illustrative error budget, assuming independent errors combined in quadrature and perfect volume and identity:
  - Plain cooked white rice: density ±12% (packing) combined with composition ±5% gives about ±13% kcal.
  - Grilled chicken breast (correctly mapped to cooked): density ±5% combined with composition (fat-driven) ±10-15% gives about ±12-16% kcal. If it is mapped to raw instead, there is an extra systematic error of about −30%.
  - Home-style Brazilian arroz + feijão with unknown oil: the ±13% above plus an oil term of 0 to +1 tbsp (+120 kcal on a roughly 400 kcal portion, about +30%) gives a skewed error of about −13% to +40%.
  - Mixed salad with dressing: density up to ±40% (lettuce type) plus an unseen dressing that can double the kcal gives an error exceeding ±50%.
  - Packaged item via OFF barcode: about ±20% (label tolerance) plus crowd-sourcing transcription errors.
- The data stage therefore adds error comparable to, not smaller than, the camera volume error for many Brazilian meals. The biggest wins are (1) forcing cooked-state and method facets in label mapping, (2) using as-eaten preparation entries (FNDDS; TBCA if licensed) that include typical oil, and (3) showing kcal and fat as ranges.

### Gaps
- No study was found that isolates the error of the lookup stage (density plus composition) from the vision stages in an end-to-end camera pipeline. The budget above is a reasoned estimate from component ranges, not a measured result.
